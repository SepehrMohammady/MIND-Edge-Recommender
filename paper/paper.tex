\begin{abstract}
Running a news recommender on the device itself keeps a reader's clicks private,
but the usual models store a word-embedding table of tens to hundreds of megabytes
per language, which no microcontroller's flash can hold. We avoid the table by
reading a title as its raw UTF-8 bytes: a small char-CNN whose 256-value
vocabulary is shared by every language. Around this encoder we search three
families of architecture (unconstrained, budget-constrained and binary-aware) and
evaluate each at full precision and with inner-layer weights at eight bits and at
one bit, measuring ranking quality on MIND against estimated flash, RAM and
energy. With eight-bit weights the constrained model scores 0.614 AUC at an
estimated 204\,KB and the unconstrained one 0.639 at 790\,KB (means over three
seeds). For reference,
NRMS with GloVe vectors scores 0.664 over three seeds, a reduced NRMS with a
random 256-dimensional table 0.604, and a per-user category histogram with no
network 0.598. At the constrained architecture, starting from weights distilled
from a frozen multilingual sentence-transformer adds 0.011 AUC (0.629 against
0.618), and training on clicks shown in a random xMIND language raises the mean
over fourteen translations from 0.557 to 0.589 at a cost of 0.020 in English.
Naive one-bit weights cost the constrained model 0.065 AUC (0.554); ReActNet
blocks started from distilled weights reach 0.587 at 194\,KB.
Standard deviations over seeds reach 0.017, so smaller gaps are not resolved. Exported as an
8-bit file, the distilled constrained encoder scores 0.622 (0.628 at full
precision) and runs in 31.2\,ms per title on an STM32H7B3I-DK and 450\,ms on a
NUCLEO-F401RE. A $\mu$NAS search under the budgets of the two boards, with the
final choice made after full training, finds encoders of 0.605 and 0.608 AUC
against 0.599 and 0.589 for the best hand-designed shapes of the same budgets;
the one for the NUCLEO-F401RE takes 60.6\,ms per title there, against 88.7\,ms
for that shape. On an Android phone the 8-bit encoder takes 0.53\,ms and about
0.95\,mJ per title. \TODO{energy on the boards}. Code
and artefacts are released.

\section{Introduction}
Running personalisation on the phone or sensor node rather than the cloud is
attractive for privacy: the record of what a user reads never leaves the device.
Two difficulties come with that setting. A
model that starts from nothing for each new user recommends poorly until it has
seen enough clicks, and the standard neural recommenders (NRMS~\cite{wu2019nrms},
NAML~\cite{wu2019naml} and their relatives) represent a title with a
word-embedding matrix. That matrix is large: GloVe-300d over 30--100k words is
already 37--120\,MB, one table per language, and none of it fits a
microcontroller's few megabytes of flash.

We study both problems offline, on MIND~\cite{wu2020mind} and its translated
extension xMIND~\cite{iana2024xmind}, and then shrink the result for the device.
The idea is to drop words and read a title as its UTF-8 bytes. A byte takes 256
values, so the vocabulary is a single 256-row table shared by every language, and
one small convolutional encoder reads Chinese, Georgian and English alike. A frozen
multilingual teacher~\cite{reimers2020multilingual} supplies distillation targets
that place a translated title near its English original; we use them to score
candidate architectures and to initialise the encoder.

We contribute three things. A byte-level content encoder removes the
embedding-table memory wall: its vocabulary is one 256-row table for every
language. Around it we benchmark three architecture searches (unconstrained,
budget-constrained and binary-aware) at three weight precisions, reporting
accuracy alongside estimated flash, RAM and energy, and search again with
$\mu$NAS under the budgets of two STM32 boards, measuring the results on the
boards. Finally we measure what one-bit weights cost at this scale and how much
of it ReActNet activations and Bi-Real shortcuts, started from distilled
weights, recover. Character-level inputs have been used
for click prediction~\cite{edizel2017char}, and vocabulary-free projections give
text classifiers of about 200\,KB~\cite{kaliamoorthi2019prado}. A search of arXiv
and IEEE Xplore (October 2026) returned no news recommender that reads raw bytes
and none evaluated on a microcontroller; the nearest on-device NAS and binary
work is on vision~\cite{lin2020mcunet,nasbnn2024,pighetti2026nasbnn}.

\section{Related Work}
Neural news recommendation on MIND is well charted. NRMS~\cite{wu2019nrms},
NAML~\cite{wu2019naml}, LSTUR, NPA and DKN encode titles and
users with attention or knowledge graphs; their published test AUC on the large
split runs from 0.65 (DKN) to 0.68 (NRMS)~\cite{wu2020mind}. Language-model
recommenders (PLM-NR, Fastformer, UNBERT) reach 0.70--0.73~\cite{wu2023survey}
but wrap a BERT-sized encoder of hundreds of megabytes, out of reach on a
microcontroller; we keep NRMS as the reference that is closest in size.
The tools we borrow are vocabulary-free character and byte
models~\cite{zhang2015charcnn,gillick2016bytes,clark2022canine}, cross-lingual
distillation~\cite{reimers2020multilingual}, memory-budgeted
NAS~\cite{lin2020mcunet}, and the binary-network line of
XNOR-Net~\cite{rastegari2016xnor}, Bi-Real~\cite{liu2018bireal} and
ReActNet~\cite{liu2020reactnet}; what is new is putting them together for on-device
multilingual news.

We treat recommendation as impression-level ranking: score candidate titles against the
titles a user has clicked, embedding both with the same encoder
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). The encoder reads a title's bytes through a 256-entry
embedding, a few depthwise-separable one-dimensional convolutions, a masked
average over positions, and a linear projection. An additive-attention encoder
summarises the user's history, and the score is the dot product of the two
vectors, trained with one positive and four negatives redrawn at every step
(AdamW). A frozen
\texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2}~\cite{wang2020minilm} supplies a
384-dimensional target for each English title, and because the xMIND titles are
translations of those same articles the distillation loss maps every translation
to that one target; a Matryoshka~\cite{kusupati2022matryoshka} term keeps the
leading coordinates informative on their own. Distillation has two uses here: it
is the fitness of the architecture search, and it initialises the encoder in the
ablation and in the one-bit model of Sect.~\ref{sec:results}. The nine models of
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} are trained on clicks from a random initialisation.

The search has three arms: one unconstrained, one held to an on-device budget
with eight-bit weights, and one held to the same budget with one-bit weights. The
space holds 120 architectures (six channel counts, five depths, four output
widths). Each arm runs an evolutionary search over it, scores a candidate by a
short distillation, and rejects candidates over budget; precision is then the
second axis. Quantisation is simulated on the weights with a straight-through
estimator, per-channel for eight bits and sign-times-scale for one. Following
XNOR-Net, the byte embedding and the outermost layers stay in full precision, and
so do all activations, so the quantised rows cover 66\%, 31\% and 24\% of the
parameters of the three architectures. For the binary arm alone we add the
ReActNet activations and Bi-Real shortcuts, binarise the block inputs and start
from the distilled weights, which is what makes one-bit training converge to
something useful. Sizes are parameter counts at each layer's precision and
multiply-accumulates are counted per layer. The energy column multiplies them by
per-operation figures: 4.6 and 0.23\,pJ for a 32-bit float and an eight-bit
integer multiply-accumulate at 45\,nm~\cite{horowitz2014}, and one sixty-fourth of
the former for a one-bit operation, the operation-count convention of binary
networks~\cite{rastegari2016xnor,liu2018bireal}. We read it only as a relative
ordering, since it ignores the memory traffic that often dominates on a real chip.

For the boards we searched again, with the lab's one-dimensional
version~\cite{capello2025unas1d} of $\mu$NAS~\cite{liberis2021unas}, because the
June space holds only channel-depth grids and its operation count was wrong. The
$\mu$NAS space is much wider: up to ten convolution blocks of standard, pointwise
or depthwise layers (kernel 3 to 7, stride 1 or 2, optional max-pooling, batch
normalisation and ReLU), optional residual branches, and a global-average-pooling
head with a 384-unit output. A candidate reads a title's bytes through the fixed
byte table of the distilled reference encoder plus a padding-mask channel, and is
distilled to the teacher on 96,000 rows in fifteen languages for at most fifteen
epochs; its error is one minus the mean validation cosine. Aging evolution (150
candidates, population 40, sample 12) trades the error against the
multiply-accumulates, eight-bit weight size and peak activation memory of the
fork's resource model, under one budget per board: 256\,KiB of weights, 128\,KiB
of activations and 2\,M multiply-accumulates per title for the STM32H7B3I-DK, and
128\,KiB, 48\,KiB and 1\,M for the NUCLEO-F401RE. The eight best candidates within
budget are trained again with three seeds under the search recipe (the recipe
choice). Because that short recipe ranks candidates differently from full
training (Sect.~\ref{sec:results}), the final choice among the same eight is made
after full distillation, by the mean validation cosine over three seeds. Both
choices use validation rows only. The 30 hand-designed shapes of the June space with a
384-wide output are trained under the same recipe and costed by the same model.
For the ranking experiments the chosen networks, the best hand-designed shape within
each budget and 64-5-384 are rebuilt in PyTorch with this input and pooling
(outputs within $2.3\times10^{-5}$ of the Keras build) and trained like the
reference encoder: distillation on all 769k training rows, then English clicks.

\section{Experimental Setup}
We use MINDsmall~\cite{wu2020mind}: fifty thousand users, about 157k training and
73k development impressions over 51k articles, together with the fourteen NLLB
translations of xMIND~\cite{iana2024xmind,nllb2022}. xMIND provides only translated
titles, keyed to the same article ids, so we reuse the English behaviour logs and
swap the text. Every recommender we train, and the
NRMS baseline, sees only English clicks, and the other languages appear only at
evaluation. The multilingual numbers are therefore a test of cross-lingual
transfer in the content encoder, not of native-language personalisation. We stay on
the small split rather than the million-user MINDlarge because the nine cells of
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} alone take 5.6 hours of training for three seeds on one
laptop; the small split has no public test set, so
we evaluate on dev with the number of epochs fixed in advance. The search runs a
population of thirty over fifteen generations, scoring each candidate by a
two-epoch distillation on twenty thousand titles. The budgets of the constrained
arms are 256\,KB of flash and 128\,KB of RAM, about eight times tighter than an
STM32H7 holds. A bound of 2\,M multiply-accumulates was also set, but the counter
of that run skipped the quantised layers, so both constrained winners (3.29\,M and
3.26\,M) exceed it. The $\mu$NAS searches of Sect.~3 enforce it with the
corrected count.
Everything runs on one laptop: an Intel Core Ultra~9 275HX with an eight-gigabyte
RTX~5070 Laptop GPU and 32\,GB of memory, PyTorch~2.12 on CUDA~13. The targets
are STM32 microcontrollers and an Android phone. Board latency, flash and RAM
come from the ST Edge AI Developer Cloud (ST Edge AI Core 4.0.1) on an
STM32H7B3I-DK (Cortex-M7, 280\,MHz) and a NUCLEO-F401RE (Cortex-M4, 84\,MHz).
Phone latency and energy come from a DOOGEE S98Pro (MediaTek Helio G96, two
Cortex-A76 and six Cortex-A55 cores, Android~12), with ONNX Runtime~1.27 inside
our reader app; the energy is read from the phone's own battery gauge, unplugged,
over 15 minutes with the encoder idle and 15 minutes with it running;
\TODO{board energy}. Every run is seeded (seeds 42, 1 and 2 for
Table~\ref{tab:matrix}; 42, 12 and 1 for Tables~\ref{tab:p1} and~\ref{tab:unas}),
uses the loop of Sect.~3, which redraws the negatives at every step, and is
reported as the mean and standard deviation over its three seeds. The June
architectures are kept; the June run that found them drew its negatives once and
did not seed the network initialisation. The downloaded data is pinned by checksum, and
the code, notebook, result files and a dependency lock are
released.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/SepehrMohammady/MIND-Edge-Recommender}}

Table~\ref{tab:matrix} collects the nine combinations (the three arms found the
architectures 256-4-384, 64-5-384 and 96-2-384, with 36.1, 3.29 and 3.26 million
multiply-accumulates per title). Two patterns emerge. Moving the inner weights
from full to eight-bit precision changes AUC by at most 0.011, inside the spread
over seeds, while the energy estimate falls 3.4 to 9.3 times and the size 1.2 to
2 times. One bit is different: it loses 0.029 to 0.065 AUC, and because the
embedding and outer layers stay in full precision its size advantage over eight
bits is small at this scale. The RAM estimate, the input
and output buffer of the widest layer at one byte per activation, is 16\,KB for
the constrained model and 64\,KB for the unconstrained one. On the laptop a title
takes 0.10--0.34\,ms on CPU (onnxruntime) and under 0.7\,ms on GPU for the three
full-precision architectures.

\input{tab_p1}
Table~\ref{tab:p1} places these numbers. Our NRMS reproduction in the reduced
configuration of the June run (a randomly initialised 256-dimensional word table;
92\% of its 7.08 million parameters are the table, 27\,MB) averages 0.604 over
three seeds with a spread of 0.010, which covers the 0.607 of the June run. In
the reference configuration shipped with MIND (GloVe-initialised 300-dimensional
table, twenty heads) NRMS reaches 0.664, in line with the 0.656 reported for this
split~\cite{mao2022digat}. Two scorers with no network set a floor: popularity
gives 0.525, and a histogram of the reader's clicks over MIND's category labels
0.598, or 0.617 with the finer subcategory labels. The reference byte-CNN trained
on English clicks scores 0.618 from a random start and 0.629 from the distilled
weights, so distillation is worth 0.011 here, less than the 0.033 of the June
ablation, which drew negatives once and trained for ten epochs. The gap to GloVe
NRMS is 0.036 with 165 times fewer parameters, and the distilled encoder is 0.011
above the subcategory histogram, which has no network at all. Feeds outside MIND
carry no such editorial labels, which is the case a content encoder is for.

Cross-lingual transfer depends on how the encoder is trained (last column of
Table~\ref{tab:p1}). Trained from a random start on English clicks it reads the
fourteen translated dev sets at 0.528 on average, close to chance; the models of
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} are of this kind. The distilled student alone, frozen under a trained user
encoder, scores 0.572 across languages against 0.573 in English, so the
distillation does carry the teacher's language alignment (the teacher itself:
0.575 against 0.589). Fine-tuning the distilled encoder on English clicks raises
English to 0.629 but leaves the translations at 0.557; showing the same clicks in
a random xMIND language instead keeps 0.589 across languages, from 0.538
(Georgian) to 0.609 (Finnish), for 0.609 in English. A trainable linear map on
the frozen teacher reaches 0.590 across languages with the whole teacher behind
it; the 68k-parameter byte-CNN comes within 0.001 of that figure.

Two further points qualify the tables. The standard deviation over seeds reaches
0.017 in Table~\ref{tab:matrix}, so we read gaps below about 0.02 as unresolved;
the June single run of each cell lies within 0.033 of the new mean, the largest
difference being the one-bit constrained model (0.521 in June). For the 3\% of impressions whose reader has
no history, a history-based score ties every candidate (0.500 by construction);
popularity gives 0.529 there and the population's category distribution 0.533. By
history length, the distilled encoder trained on English clicks scores 0.615 for
readers with one to five clicks, 0.633 for six to twenty and 0.638 beyond, against
0.622, 0.664 and 0.688 for GloVe NRMS: the gap widens with history.

Binarising the inner weights the naive way costs the constrained model 0.065
AUC (0.554 over three seeds). Replacing its blocks with full binary
convolutions, ReActNet activations and Bi-Real shortcuts, and starting from
distilled weights, gives $0.587\pm0.012$ over the same seeds (0.572 in the single
June run) at an estimated 194\,KB: 0.032 above naive binarisation, 0.018 below the
reduced NRMS and 0.042 below the distilled encoder at full precision
(Table~\ref{tab:p1}). On the accuracy-versus-footprint
view (Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}) eight-bit Micro-NAS (0.614, 204\,KB, 3.2\uJ) has
the lowest cost at the accuracy of the reduced NRMS, while unconstrained
eight-bit leads on accuracy (0.639, 790\,KB).

For the boards, the trained constrained encoder (distilled start, English clicks)
was exported to ONNX without its byte-table lookup, which the caller performs, and
quantised to an 8-bit QDQ model with ONNX Runtime (per-channel 8-bit weights and
8-bit inputs to every convolution and to the output layer, calibrated on 512
training titles; activation functions and averages stay in floating point). The
8-bit file scores 0.622 AUC against 0.628 for the same weights at full precision
(0.624 against 0.629 over three seeds). On the
STM32H7B3I-DK it runs in 31.2\,ms per title, 2.5 times faster than at full
precision (77.0\,ms), with 53.2\,KB of weights, 77.7\,KB of flash including the
runtime and 49.4\,KB of RAM, plus the 64.3\,KB byte table; on the NUCLEO-F401RE it
takes 450\,ms, no faster than full precision there (444\,ms). The board's own check
of the generated code against the reference model gave zero error.
On the phone the 8-bit file of the encoder trained on mixed-language clicks, the
one built into our reader app, computes the same vectors as on the laptop
(largest difference $4.9\times10^{-7}$ on titles in five scripts) and takes
0.53\,ms per title on one core (median over 2,000 calls, from the title's bytes to
its vector; 0.48\,ms with two threads); the full-precision file takes 0.59\,ms.
At this size eight bits buy no speed on the phone. The file scores 0.607 AUC in
English and 0.592 over the fourteen translations. Encoding in a loop on one core
(1,806 titles per second), the unplugged phone draws 2.86\,W against 1.15\,W with
the encoder idle and the screen on, about 0.95\,mJ per title. The phone reports
only an averaged current and a charge counter in steps of 1\,\%, which give 0.83
and 1.6\,mJ, so we read the cost as roughly one millijoule per title.

In the app the encoder also feeds the ranking of the feed. The app orders
articles by a rule it learns on the phone: a weight per topic that grows with
every opened or read article, plus terms for drift, freshness and unread items.
We ported the rule to Python (the port's scores equal the app's on 600 events
and 200 articles) and replayed the MINDsmall clicks through it in time order,
scoring each impression before the rule learns from its clicks, with the MIND
category as topic and the first appearance of an article in place of its
publication time. The content score of a candidate is the dot product of its
vector from the app's 8-bit file with the user vector that the user encoder of
the same checkpoint pools from the last 50 clicked titles; it enters the rule
standardised within the impression and multiplied by a weight. The encoder was
trained on the training clicks, so we chose the weight on the earlier half of
the dev impressions and report the later half (36,576 impressions). There the
rule alone scores 0.600 AUC and the content score alone 0.594; their sum, with
the chosen weight of 4, scores 0.624, 0.024 above the rule (95\,\% interval
0.021--0.027 over 1,000 resamples of the 28,445 users). With the subcategory as
topic the rule scores 0.606 and the sum 0.632 (weight 2). The app now ranks
with this sum and a weight of 2, the best on the earlier half averaged over
both topic definitions.

are, within the H7 budget, three convolutions (98, 26 and 94 channels, with one
max-pooling) at 2.00\,M multiply-accumulates and, within the F401 budget, a
strided depthwise and a strided five-wide convolution at 0.65\,M. Both lead the

AUC within the H7 budget and 0.591 against 0.589 within the F401 budget,
differences smaller than the spread over seeds (0.543 against 0.547 and 0.537
against 0.538 across the fourteen translations). Trained on clicks shown in a
random language, as in Table~\ref{tab:p1}, the two recipe choices read the
translations at 0.562 and 0.556 and English at 0.571 and 0.549. Full
distillation reorders the eight finalists of each search: the recipe choices fall
to fifth and fourth place, and the final choices are candidate 140 (H7, 1.93\,M)
and candidate 142 (F401, 0.84\,M, with one residual branch). These reach 0.605
and 0.608 AUC, ahead of the hand-designed shapes by 0.006, inside the spread over
seeds, and by 0.019. All of them stay below 64-5-384, which fits neither
operation budget and scores 0.618 in this form and 0.629 in its original one.
Under the search recipe the H7 recipe choice was 0.012 below 64-5-384 in teacher
cosine, after full distillation 0.067: a short distillation is a weak predictor
of how far a network gets with full training.

On the NUCLEO-F401RE the recipe's F401 choice is the fastest of the seven
encoders: 32.1\,ms per title, against 88.7\,ms for 32-5-384 and 427\,ms for
64-5-384. It has a third fewer multiply-accumulates than 32-5-384 but runs 2.8
times faster, at 4.1 cycles per multiply-accumulate against 7.2: in 32-5-384 the
depthwise layers take 22 cycles per multiply-accumulate and the first pointwise
layer, over the 65 input channels, 8.8. The final F401 choice, larger and more
accurate, takes 60.6\,ms there, 1.5 times less than 32-5-384, and is the fastest
on the STM32H7B3I-DK (12.1\,ms). Within the H7 budget the hand-designed 64-2-384
is faster on that board (16.6\,ms) than both searched choices (18.7 and
28.4\,ms). All in-budget encoders keep their ranking accuracy as 8-bit files. Quantising every
operator rather than only the convolutions and the output layer kept the accuracy
but made no file faster; on the H7 the code generator then fused one convolution
with its max-pooling into an integer kernel that took 20.2\,ms alone.

\section{Limitations}
The main gap is hardware. We timed the matrix models on the laptop and estimated
size, RAM and energy from parameter and operation counts; only the deployed
encoders ran on the boards and the phone; the phone's energy comes from its own
battery gauge, and the boards' energy has not been measured yet. In the matrix the
quantisation is simulated on weights only: activations, the byte embedding and the
outer layers stay in floating point, so the eight-bit and one-bit rows may
overstate accuracy and their sizes are not those of an 8-bit file; for the
constrained encoder the 8-bit file costs 0.004 AUC on average over three seeds and for the
in-budget encoders of Table~\ref{tab:unas} at most 0.003. Eight-bit inference
runs on STM32 boards through
quantised ONNX models and ST Edge AI, as measured above. The one-bit path needs a
binary runtime such as CBin-NN~\cite{sakr2024cbinnn} or Larq Compute
Engine~\cite{bannink2021lce}, and the Cortex-M7 has no popcount instruction, so we
make no speed claim for binary and treat it only as a way to save space. The
energy figure ignores SRAM and memory traffic, which can dominate on a Cortex-M.
The June constrained search enforced its flash and RAM budgets but not the
operation budget. The $\mu$NAS searches score a
candidate by a short distillation, whose ranking did not survive full training
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results}); the final choice after full distillation of the eight
finalists changed both choices, and a longer fitness inside the search would
cost more search time. Even after mixed-language
training the translations stay 0.020 below English and 0.039 below the
English-only encoder. Finally, a byte sequence is about five times longer than the word
sequence, trading flash for computation, and all of this is on the small MIND
split. The replay through the app's ranking rule has clicks only: MIND records
no reading time or scrolling, from which the app also learns, and no
publication time, and no reader has used the content-aware ranking yet.

\section{Ethics and Conclusion}
MIND and xMIND are used under their non-commercial research licences; the work
touches no personal data beyond the public logs, and the translations are
machine-generated. Replacing the word-embedding table with a byte-level encoder
removes the largest item of a news recommender's storage and gives every language
the same 256-row vocabulary. With eight-bit weights the constrained encoder
