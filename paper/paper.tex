\begin{abstract}
Running a news recommender on the device itself keeps a reader's clicks private,
but the usual models store a word-embedding table of tens to hundreds of megabytes
per language, which no microcontroller's flash can hold. We avoid the table by
reading a title as its raw UTF-8 bytes: a small char-CNN whose 256-value
vocabulary is shared by every language. Around this encoder we search three
families of architecture (unconstrained, budget-constrained and binary-aware) and
evaluate each at full precision and with inner-layer weights at eight bits and at
one bit, measuring ranking quality on MIND against estimated flash, RAM and
energy. With eight-bit weights the constrained model scores 0.610 AUC at an
estimated 204\,KB and the unconstrained one 0.647 at 790\,KB. For reference,
NRMS with GloVe vectors scores 0.664 over three seeds, a reduced NRMS with a
random 256-dimensional table 0.604, and a per-user category histogram with no
network 0.598. At the constrained architecture, starting from weights distilled
from a frozen multilingual sentence-transformer adds 0.011 AUC (0.629 against
0.618), and training on clicks shown in a random xMIND language raises the mean
over fourteen translations from 0.557 to 0.589 at a cost of 0.020 in English. A
naive one-bit model stays near chance (0.521); ReActNet blocks started from the
distilled weights bring it to 0.572 at 194\,KB. Seeded reruns differ from the
matrix run by up to 0.02 AUC, so smaller gaps are not resolved. \TODO{latency and
energy on STM32 boards and an Android phone}. Code and artefacts are released.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Running personalisation on the phone or sensor node rather than the cloud is
attractive for privacy: the record of what a user reads never leaves the device.
Two difficulties come with that setting. A
model that starts from nothing for each new user recommends poorly until it has
seen enough clicks, and the standard neural recommenders (NRMS~\cite{wu2019nrms},
NAML~\cite{wu2019naml} and their relatives) represent a title with a
word-embedding matrix. That matrix is large: GloVe-300d over 30--100k words is
already 37--120\,MB, one table per language, and none of it fits a
microcontroller's few megabytes of flash.

We study both problems offline, on MIND~\cite{wu2020mind} and its translated
extension xMIND~\cite{iana2024xmind}, and then shrink the result for the device.
The idea is to drop words and read a title as its UTF-8 bytes. A byte takes 256
values, so the vocabulary is a single 256-row table shared by every language, and
one small convolutional encoder reads Chinese, Georgian and English alike. A frozen
multilingual teacher~\cite{reimers2020multilingual} supplies distillation targets
that place a translated title near its English original; we use them to score
candidate architectures and to initialise the encoder.

We contribute three things. A byte-level content encoder removes the
embedding-table memory wall: its vocabulary is one 256-row table for every
language. Around it we benchmark three architecture searches (unconstrained,
budget-constrained and binary-aware) at three weight precisions, reporting
accuracy alongside estimated flash, RAM and energy. Finally we bring the one-bit
case from near chance to 0.572 AUC with ReActNet activations and Bi-Real
shortcuts started from distilled weights. Character-level inputs have been used
for click prediction~\cite{edizel2017char}, and vocabulary-free projections give
text classifiers of about 200\,KB~\cite{kaliamoorthi2019prado}. A search of arXiv
and IEEE Xplore (October 2026) returned no news recommender that reads raw bytes
and none evaluated on a microcontroller; the nearest on-device NAS and binary
work is on vision~\cite{lin2020mcunet,nasbnn2024,pighetti2026nasbnn}.

\section{Related Work}
Neural news recommendation on MIND is well charted. NRMS~\cite{wu2019nrms},
NAML~\cite{wu2019naml}, LSTUR, NPA and DKN encode titles and
users with attention or knowledge graphs; their published test AUC on the large
split runs from 0.65 (DKN) to 0.68 (NRMS)~\cite{wu2020mind}. Language-model
recommenders (PLM-NR, Fastformer, UNBERT) reach 0.70--0.73~\cite{wu2023survey}
but wrap a BERT-sized encoder of hundreds of megabytes, out of reach on a
microcontroller; we keep NRMS as the reference that is closest in size.
The tools we borrow are vocabulary-free character and byte
models~\cite{zhang2015charcnn,gillick2016bytes,clark2022canine}, cross-lingual
distillation~\cite{reimers2020multilingual}, memory-budgeted
NAS~\cite{lin2020mcunet}, and the binary-network line of
XNOR-Net~\cite{rastegari2016xnor}, Bi-Real~\cite{liu2018bireal} and
ReActNet~\cite{liu2020reactnet}; what is new is putting them together for on-device
multilingual news.

We treat recommendation as impression-level ranking: score candidate titles against the
titles a user has clicked, embedding both with the same encoder
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). The encoder reads a title's bytes through a 256-entry
embedding, a few depthwise-separable one-dimensional convolutions, a masked
average over positions, and a linear projection. An additive-attention encoder
summarises the user's history, and the score is the dot product of the two
vectors, trained with one positive and four negatives redrawn at every step
(AdamW). A frozen
\texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2}~\cite{wang2020minilm} supplies a
384-dimensional target for each English title, and because the xMIND titles are
translations of those same articles the distillation loss maps every translation
to that one target; a Matryoshka~\cite{kusupati2022matryoshka} term keeps the
leading coordinates informative on their own. Distillation has two uses here: it
is the fitness of the architecture search, and it initialises the encoder in the
ablation and in the one-bit model of Sect.~\ref{sec:results}. The nine models of
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} are trained on clicks from a random initialisation.

The search has three arms: one unconstrained, one held to an on-device budget
with eight-bit weights, and one held to the same budget with one-bit weights. The
space holds 120 architectures (six channel counts, five depths, four output
widths). Each arm runs an evolutionary search over it, scores a candidate by a
short distillation, and rejects candidates over budget; precision is then the
second axis. Quantisation is simulated on the weights with a straight-through
estimator, per-channel for eight bits and sign-times-scale for one. Following
XNOR-Net, the byte embedding and the outermost layers stay in full precision, and
so do all activations, so the quantised rows cover 66\%, 31\% and 24\% of the
parameters of the three architectures. For the binary arm alone we add the
ReActNet activations and Bi-Real shortcuts, binarise the block inputs and start
from the distilled weights, which is what makes one-bit training converge to
something useful. Sizes are parameter counts at each layer's precision and
multiply-accumulates are counted per layer. The energy column multiplies them by
per-operation figures: 4.6 and 0.23\,pJ for a 32-bit float and an eight-bit
integer multiply-accumulate at 45\,nm~\cite{horowitz2014}, and one sixty-fourth of
the former for a one-bit operation, the operation-count convention of binary
networks~\cite{rastegari2016xnor,liu2018bireal}. We read it only as a relative
ordering, since it ignores the memory traffic that often dominates on a real chip.

\section{Experimental Setup}
We use MINDsmall~\cite{wu2020mind}: fifty thousand users, about 157k training and
73k development impressions over 51k articles, together with the fourteen NLLB
translations of xMIND~\cite{iana2024xmind,nllb2022}. xMIND provides only translated
titles, keyed to the same article ids, so we reuse the English behaviour logs and
swap the text. Every recommender we train, and the
NRMS baseline, sees only English clicks, and the other languages appear only at
evaluation. The multilingual numbers are therefore a test of cross-lingual
transfer in the content encoder, not of native-language personalisation. We stay on
the small split rather than the million-user MINDlarge because the full study ---
three arms, three precisions, the search and the fifteen-language evaluation ---
already takes 8.2 hours on one laptop; the small split has no public test set, so
we evaluate on dev with the number of epochs fixed in advance. The search runs a
population of thirty over fifteen generations, scoring each candidate by a
two-epoch distillation on twenty thousand titles. The budgets of the constrained
arms are 256\,KB of flash and 128\,KB of RAM, about eight times tighter than an
STM32H7 holds. A bound of 2\,M multiply-accumulates was also set, but the counter
of that run skipped the quantised layers, so both constrained winners (3.29\,M and
3.26\,M) exceed it; \TODO{rerun the constrained search with the corrected count}.
Everything runs on one laptop: an Intel Core Ultra~9 275HX with an eight-gigabyte
RTX~5070 Laptop GPU and 32\,GB of memory, PyTorch~2.12 on CUDA~13. The intended
targets are STM32 microcontrollers and an Android phone;
\TODO{board and phone measurements}, and this draft reports laptop timings and
analytical estimates only. Negative sampling and the search are seeded; network
initialisation in the matrix run was not, which the seeded reruns of
Sect.~\ref{sec:results} quantify. The downloaded data is pinned by checksum, and
the code, notebook, result files and a dependency lock are
released.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/SepehrMohammady/MIND-Edge-Recommender}}
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} comes from the June run, which drew the negatives once; the
experiments of Table~\ref{tab:p1} use the loop of Sect.~3, which redraws them at
every step, and report the mean and standard deviation over three seeds.

Table~\ref{tab:matrix} collects the nine combinations (the three arms found the
architectures 256-4-384, 64-5-384 and 96-2-384, with 36.1, 3.29 and 3.26 million
multiply-accumulates per title). Two patterns emerge. Moving the inner weights
from full to eight-bit precision changes AUC by at most 0.014, inside the
run-to-run variation reported below, while the energy estimate falls 3.4 to 9.3
times and the size 1.2 to 2 times. One bit is different: it loses five to nine
AUC points, and because the embedding and outer layers stay in full precision its
size advantage over eight bits is small at this scale. The RAM estimate, the input
and output buffer of the widest layer at one byte per activation, is 16\,KB for
the constrained model and 64\,KB for the unconstrained one. On the laptop a title
takes 0.10--0.34\,ms on CPU (onnxruntime) and under 0.7\,ms on GPU for the three
full-precision architectures.

Table~\ref{tab:p1} places these numbers. Our NRMS reproduction in the reduced
configuration of the June run (a randomly initialised 256-dimensional word table;
92\% of its 7.08 million parameters are the table, 27\,MB) averages 0.604 over
three seeds with a spread of 0.010, which covers the 0.607 of the matrix run. In
the reference configuration shipped with MIND (GloVe-initialised 300-dimensional
table, twenty heads) NRMS reaches 0.664, in line with the 0.656 reported for this
split~\cite{mao2022digat}. Two scorers with no network set a floor: popularity
gives 0.525, and a histogram of the reader's clicks over MIND's category labels
0.598, or 0.617 with the finer subcategory labels. The reference byte-CNN trained
on English clicks scores 0.618 from a random start and 0.629 from the distilled
weights, so distillation is worth 0.011 here, less than the 0.033 of the June
ablation, which drew negatives once and trained for ten epochs. The gap to GloVe
NRMS is 0.036 with 165 times fewer parameters, and the distilled encoder is 0.011
above the subcategory histogram, which has no network at all. Feeds outside MIND
carry no such editorial labels, which is the case a content encoder is for.

Cross-lingual transfer depends on how the encoder is trained (last column of
Table~\ref{tab:p1}). Trained from a random start on English clicks it reads the
fourteen translated dev sets at 0.528 on average, close to chance; the June matrix
models are of this kind. The distilled student alone, frozen under a trained user
encoder, scores 0.572 across languages against 0.573 in English, so the
distillation does carry the teacher's language alignment (the teacher itself:
0.575 against 0.589). Fine-tuning the distilled encoder on English clicks raises
English to 0.629 but leaves the translations at 0.557; showing the same clicks in
a random xMIND language instead keeps 0.589 across languages, from 0.538
(Georgian) to 0.609 (Finnish), for 0.609 in English. A trainable linear map on
the frozen teacher reaches 0.590 across languages with the whole teacher behind
it; the 68k-parameter byte-CNN comes within 0.001 of that figure.

Two further points qualify the tables. Three seeded reruns of the eight-bit
constrained model score 0.630, 0.622 and 0.621; the matrix run, whose
initialisation was not seeded, scored 0.610, so we read gaps below about 0.02 in
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} as unresolved. For the 3\% of impressions whose reader has
no history, a history-based score ties every candidate (0.500 by construction);
popularity gives 0.529 there and the population's category distribution 0.533. By
history length, the distilled encoder trained on English clicks scores 0.615 for
readers with one to five clicks, 0.633 for six to twenty and 0.638 beyond, against
0.622, 0.664 and 0.688 for GloVe NRMS: the gap widens with history.

Binarising the inner weights the naive way
leaves the constrained model near chance (0.521). Replacing its blocks with full
binary convolutions, ReActNet activations and Bi-Real shortcuts, and starting from
distilled weights, gives 0.572 at an estimated 194\,KB, 0.035 below the reduced
NRMS. The 0.05 gain exceeds the run-to-run variation; three seeded runs of this
model on a reduced training budget have a standard deviation of 0.012. On the
accuracy-versus-footprint view (Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}) eight-bit Micro-NAS (0.610,
204\,KB, 3.2\uJ) has the lowest cost at the accuracy of the reduced NRMS, while
unconstrained eight-bit leads on accuracy (0.647, 790\,KB).

\section{Limitations}
The main gap is hardware. We timed the full-precision models on the laptop and
estimated size, RAM and energy from parameter and operation counts, but ran
nothing on a microcontroller or a phone. The quantisation is simulated on weights
only: activations, the byte embedding and the outer layers stay in floating point,
so the eight-bit and one-bit rows may overstate accuracy and their sizes are not
those of an integer-only file; \TODO{full-integer export, with accuracy measured
from the exported model}. Eight-bit inference has a direct path to STM32 boards
through quantised ONNX or TFLite models and ST Edge AI. The one-bit path needs a
binary runtime such as CBin-NN~\cite{sakr2024cbinnn} or Larq Compute
Engine~\cite{bannink2021lce}, and the Cortex-M7 has no popcount instruction, so we
make no speed claim for binary and treat it only as a way to save space. The
energy figure ignores SRAM and memory traffic, which can dominate on a Cortex-M.
The constrained search enforced its flash and RAM budgets but not the operation
budget, and the precision matrix predates the training loop of
Table~\ref{tab:p1}, so it will be rerun with it. Even after mixed-language
training the translations stay 0.020 below English and 0.039 below the
English-only encoder. Finally, a byte sequence is about five times longer than the word
sequence, trading flash for computation, and all of this is on the small MIND
split.

\section{Ethics and Conclusion}
MIND and xMIND are used under their non-commercial research licences; the work
touches no personal data beyond the public logs, and the translations are
machine-generated. Replacing the word-embedding table with a byte-level encoder
removes the largest item of a news recommender's storage and gives every language
the same 256-row vocabulary. With eight-bit weights the constrained encoder
reaches 0.610 AUC at an estimated 204\,KB; the one-bit model, started from
distilled weights, reaches 0.572 at 194\,KB; trained on clicks shown in a random
language, the distilled byte-CNN reads fourteen languages at 0.589 AUC on average.

and a one-bit runtime} --- that is where we take it next.
